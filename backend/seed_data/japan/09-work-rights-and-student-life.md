# Studying in Japan — Work Rights and Student Life

Adjusting to daily life in Japan involves balancing academic requirements with part-time work and finding suitable accommodation. Japan offers an immersive cultural experience and safe environment, but students must strictly abide by immigration regulations regarding work rights. As of June 2026, the Japanese government monitors student work hours via electronic tax and bank records to prevent violations of visa regulations [Source: Immigration Services Agency of Japan — https://www.isa.go.jp/en/].

---

## Work Rights and Part-Time Employment Rules

To work legally in Japan, international students must secure a specific permit and follow strict regulations:

* **The Work Permit (Shikakugai Katsudo Kyoka):** Students are not allowed to work immediately upon arrival. They must obtain "Permission to Engage in Activity Other Than That Permitted Under the Status of Residence." The easiest way is to apply at the airport immigration counter upon arrival, where they will receive a stamp on the back of their Residence Card.
* **Hours Limit:** The permit allows students to work up to **28 hours per week** while school is in session. During official school vacations (such as summer, winter, and spring breaks), this limit increases to **40 hours per week** (up to 8 hours per day) [Source: Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan — https://www.mhlw.go.jp/english/].
* **Strict Prohibitions on Adult Entertainment:** Students are legally barred from working in the adult entertainment industry (Fuzoku). This includes jobs at host/hostess clubs, pachinko parlors, massage parlors, love hotels, or even working as a dishwasher or cleaner in these establishments. Violating this rule leads to immediate arrest, detention, and deportation.
* **Attendance Requirement:** A student's attendance rate at their school must remain high (typically above 80–90%). If attendance falls due to excessive work, immigration will deny visa extensions.

## Job Search and Wages

Part-time jobs (known as *arubaito* or *baito*) are readily available, especially in urban centers:

* **Common Job Sectors:** Convenience stores (*konbini*), food factories (sorting and packing), restaurant kitchens or hall service, hotel room cleaning, and English tutoring.
* **Language Requirements:** Kitchen and factory work require basic Japanese (N5/N4). Customer service roles in convenience stores and restaurants require conversational Japanese (N3/N2).
* **Wages:** The hourly wage varies by region and is tied to the minimum wage. In Tokyo, wages range from **JPY 1,150 to JPY 1,400 per hour**. In other major cities like Osaka, Fukuoka, or Nagoya, they range from **JPY 1,000 to JPY 1,200 per hour** [Source: Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare of Japan — https://www.mhlw.go.jp/english/].

## Accommodation and Daily Life

Securing housing is one of the first tasks for students after arrival:

* **School Dormitories:** The most convenient option for the first six months. They are usually pre-furnished and affordable (JPY 20,000 to JPY 45,000 per month), though they often have strict curfews and shared facilities.
* **Share Houses:** Private rooms with shared kitchens and bathrooms. They are popular for social interaction and do not require expensive upfront fees.
* **Private Apartments:** Renting an apartment independently is complex. It requires key money (*Reikin* - non-refundable gift to the landlord), security deposit (*Shikikin*), and a Japanese guarantor or guarantor company. The monthly rent ranges from JPY 40,000 to JPY 80,000.
* **Banking and Communication:** Opening a bank account at Japan Post Bank (Yucho Bank) is recommended for new arrivals, as other commercial banks require at least 6 months of residency. A mobile phone connection can be secured using a Residence Card with the registered address.
